# Virtual Socrates — Question Bank & Method Pack

Ready-to-use material for the `virtual-socrates` skill. Load when a dialogue needs a
sharper question, a way out of a stalled exchange, or an authentic Socratic touch. Ask
*one or two* questions at a time — this is a menu, not a script.

## The six types of Socratic questions (Paul & Elder)

### 1. Clarification
- "What do you mean when you say ___?"
- "Could you put that another way?"
- "Can you give me an example?"
- "Are you saying ___ or ___? Which is closer?"
- "How does this relate to what we were discussing?"

### 2. Probing assumptions
- "What are you assuming here?"
- "What must be true for that to hold?"
- "Is that assumption always the case, or only sometimes?"
- "Did you choose that assumption, or inherit it?"
- "What if the opposite were true?"

### 3. Probing reasons and evidence
- "What leads you to believe that?"
- "How do you know?"
- "What would change your mind?"
- "Is this the cause, or just something that happened alongside it?"
- "What's the weakest link in that reasoning?"

### 4. Viewpoints and perspectives
- "How would someone who disagrees see this?"
- "What's the strongest version of the opposing case?"
- "Whose interests does this framing serve?"
- "Is there another way to look at it?"
- "Why is this view better than the alternative?"

### 5. Implications and consequences
- "If that's true, what follows?"
- "What are you implying by that?"
- "Where does this lead if we take it to its end?"
- "What would happen if everyone did this?"
- "Does that fit with what you said earlier?"

### 6. Questions about the question
- "Why does this question matter to you?"
- "Is this the real question, or a symptom of another one?"
- "What did the question assume before we even answered it?"
- "How could we tell if we'd answered it well?"

## The elenchus workflow

Elenchus is a *cooperative* test of consistency, not a debate to win.

1. **Elicit a thesis** — get the user to state a claim clearly ("Courage is never being
   afraid.").
2. **Secure agreement to further premises** — points the user already accepts ("A soldier
   who feels fear but stands his ground — is he courageous?").
3. **Show tension** — reveal that the premises conflict with the thesis (the brave soldier
   *does* feel fear, so the definition is too strong).
4. **Revise** — invite a better formulation ("Perhaps courage is acting well *despite*
   fear."), then test again.

The aim is a definition or belief that survives its own examples.

## Aporia — using productive puzzlement

When an easy answer breaks down and the user feels stuck, that is **aporia**, and it is
progress, not failure. Do not leave them stranded:
- Name what became clearer: "We've learned courage isn't the absence of fear."
- Name the sharper question that remains: "So the question is now: what makes acting-with-
  fear *courageous* rather than reckless?"
- Offer to continue or to land on a working definition for now.

## Authentic Socratic sayings (use sparingly, and explain)

- "The only true wisdom is in knowing you know nothing." — model intellectual humility.
- "The unexamined life is not worth living." (Apology) — the value of self-inquiry.
- "I cannot teach anybody anything; I can only make them think." — the maieutic (midwife)
  stance: help the user deliver their own insight.
- "Wonder is the beginning of wisdom." — treat puzzlement as a starting point.

Do not invent quotations. If unsure a line is genuinely Socratic, paraphrase the idea
instead of attributing a quote.

## Common thinking traps worth probing (gently)

- **Equivocation** — a key word shifts meaning mid-argument ("free" as in cost vs freedom).
- **False dilemma** — only two options presented when more exist (the worked example's
  "employee vs founder").
- **Loaded/leading question** — the question smuggles in an answer ("Why is X obviously
  bad?").
- **Appeal to popularity / authority** — "everyone says" or "an expert said" standing in
  for a reason.
- **Hidden normative leap** — sliding from "is" to "ought" without noticing.
- **Motivated reasoning** — a conclusion chosen first, reasons assembled after. Probe by
  asking what evidence *would* change their mind.

## Guardrails for the questioner

- **Steelman first.** Test the strongest version of the user's view, never a distortion.
- **Open, not leading.** Ask questions you don't already "know" the answer to; let the
  insight be theirs.
- **One or two at a time.** A wall of questions is interrogation, not inquiry.
- **Know when to stop questioning.** If the user needs a fact or a decision, give it, and
  name the assumption it rests on.
